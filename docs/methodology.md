# Methodology — The Price of Fairness

## 1. Problem statement

Risk pricing is regulated because it is consequential: the premium a driver
pays is a financial decision made by a model. Regulators increasingly ask
whether pricing models discriminate against protected groups, and some
jurisdictions (e.g., the EU for gender) ban rating variables outright. This
project turns that debate into a measurable question:

> What does fairness actually cost — in predictive accuracy, in calibration,
> and in who pays more?

We answer it in four steps: build a risk-pricing pipeline, audit it for
fairness, constrain it to satisfy a fairness definition, and trace the
accuracy-fairness frontier with the resulting cross-subsidies.

## 2. Data

The analysis uses a synthetic policy/claim dataset generated by
[`data/generate_data.py`](../data/generate_data.py) (10,000-policy committed
sample; 100,000-policy full file reproducible with fixed seed 42).

Synthetic data is a deliberate choice: the *ground truth is known*. Protected
attributes (gender, territory) genuinely affect risk by construction, so the
fairness metrics have a real signal to find and every result is auditable.

Claim process:

* **Frequency** — Poisson with rate `exp(-2.30 + gender + territory +
  age_risk + miles + experience)` times exposure. Age enters as a U-shape
  (`0.35·|age−45|/10`): youngest and oldest drivers are riskiest. Gender
  shifts the log rate by −0.20 (F) / +0.15 (M); territory by −0.25 (A),
  0 (B), +0.30 (C).
* **Severity** — Gamma(shape=2) with mean `$3,000·exp(0.25·(age−45)/15 +
  territory_sev + vehicle_sev)`: older drivers, territory C, and
  trucks/SUVs cost more per claim.

Resulting base rates (committed sample): F claim rate 10.6% vs M 13.5%;
mean loss per policy $485.

## 3. Pricing pipeline

### 3.1 Frequency model — Poisson GLM with age splines

Claim counts are modeled as

```
ClaimNb_i ~ Poisson(exp(x_i'β + log Exposure_i))
```

a log-link GLM with a log-exposure offset, so fitted values are annual claim
rates per policy-year. With an intercept, the Poisson score equations
guarantee `Σ fitted = Σ observed` — the model reproduces the overall claim
rate exactly (0.130 in the sample).

**Model improvement (important).** The first version used a *linear* age
term, which cannot represent the U-shaped risk curve. The result was severe
age mispricing:

| Age band | Linear age (pred/actual) | Age spline (pred/actual) |
|---|---:|---:|
| 18–25 | 0.63 | 0.97 |
| 25–35 | 1.06 | 1.05 |
| 35–45 | 1.49 | 1.01 |
| 45–55 | 1.57 | 0.98 |
| 55–65 | 1.18 | 0.95 |
| 65–80 | 0.78 | 1.01 |

The final model uses natural cubic B-splines (`bs(age, df=4)`), fixing age
calibration to 0.95–1.05. A regression test
(`test_frequency_model_calibrated_by_age_band`) locks this in. This is a
real-world lesson: check calibration on every dimension, not just the rating
segments you care about.

An implementation note: `statsmodels`' `predict()` drops the exposure offset
when given new data, silently inflating predicted premiums by ~33% until
fixed. [`predict_frequency`](../src/models.py) re-applies the offset and the
tests guard the identity.

### 3.2 Severity model — Gamma GLM

Severity *per claim* (total loss ÷ claim count) is modeled with a Gamma
family and log link. Modeling per-claim severity rather than
total-given-claim keeps the decomposition exact:

```
E[total loss] = E[claim count] × E[severity per claim]
```

The severity model is well calibrated across age bands (0.98–1.03), because
the generating process is linear in age; no spline is needed here.

### 3.3 Pure premium and validation

```
pure premium_i = predicted frequency_i × predicted severity per claim_i
```

* **Calibration:** predicted vs empirical premium by gender × territory
  ranges 0.94–1.05 (sample).
* **Holdout validation:** 80/20 split; total predicted $965K vs actual
  $1,004K → loss ratio 1.04.
* **Poisson dispersion:** Pearson dispersion 1.02 (target 1.0).

### 3.4 Interpretability (effect sizes)

GLMs are transparent by design: each `exp(coef)` is the multiplicative
effect of a feature on claim rate or severity, with 95% confidence
intervals. The chart below (also saved as `results/effect_sizes.png`) is
the explainability artifact a pricing committee or regulator asks for.

## 4. Fairness audit

Definitions (all computed by [`src/fairness.py`](../src/fairness.py)):

* **Base rate** — true positive-outcome rate per group (claim rate).
* **Demographic parity** — group mean premium equal to the overall mean.
* **Equalized odds** — at a common threshold, TPR and FPR equal across
  groups.
* **Calibration parity** — mean predicted premium equals mean actual loss
  in every group.
* **Premium shift** — group mean premium relative to the cheapest segment.

Sample results (gender):

| Metric | F | M |
|---|---:|---:|
| Claim rate | 10.6% | 13.5% |
| Mean predicted premium | $430 (0.88×) | $547 (1.12×) |
| TPR at threshold = mean score | 0.50 | 0.68 |
| FPR | 0.29 | 0.46 |
| Calibration (pred/actual) | 1.010 | 0.997 |

The baseline is calibrated but fails demographic parity and equalized odds —
exactly the configuration where the Chouldechova impossibility binds.

## 5. Constrained pricing and the frontier

### 5.1 Formulation

Let `p_i` be the baseline premium and `λ_g` a multiplier per segment
(gender × territory). Constrained premiums are `p'_i = λ_{g(i)} p_i`,
chosen as the solution of a convex quadratic program:

```
min_λ   (1/n) Σ_i (p'_i − y_i)²
s.t.    | mean(p'_i | h) − μ | ≤ ε·μ          for every parity group h
        Σ_i p'_i = Σ_i p_i                     (budget neutrality)
        λ_g ∈ [0.2, 2.5]
```

where `μ` is the overall mean premium and `ε` the allowed parity gap.
Solved exactly with OSQP ([`src/frontier.py`](../src/frontier.py)); sweeping
`ε` from the baseline gap to 0 traces the frontier.

We also report two actuarial metrics: the **aggregate loss ratio**
(Σ y / Σ p′) — held at ~1.00 by budget neutrality — and the **per-segment
loss-ratio gap** (max − min of Σ y / Σ p′ across parity groups), which
measures how much parity distorts cross-subsidies in insurance terms.

### 5.2 Results — full segment parity

At `ε = 0` every segment pays the same mean premium ($486.50):

| Segment | Baseline | Full parity | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| F/A | $273.29 | $486.50 | +78.0% |
| M/A | $354.16 | $486.50 | +37.4% |
| F/B | $392.53 | $486.50 | +23.9% |
| M/B | $491.40 | $486.50 | −1.0% |
| F/C | $721.74 | $486.50 | −32.6% |
| M/C | $918.68 | $486.50 | −47.1% |

**Cost: +2.14% MSE; 35.3% of premium volume changes hands.**
Loss-ratio gap widens from **0.13** (baseline) to **1.30** (full parity);
the aggregate loss ratio stays 1.00 by construction.

### 5.3 Results — gender parity only (EU-style ban)

Constraining only the two gender means:

**Cost: +0.14% MSE; 11.9% of premium volume changes hands.**
Loss-ratio gap widens from **0.02** to **0.25**.

Territory still prices risk; banning the single variable costs an order of
magnitude less than flattening all segments.

### 5.4 The calibration trade-off

Imposing parity breaks calibration by construction. Premium/actual ratios at
full parity:

| Segment | Baseline | Full parity |
|---|---:|---:|
| F/A | 1.04 | 1.85 (overcharged) |
| F/B | 1.05 | 1.30 |
| F/C | 0.97 | 0.65 (undercharged) |
| M/A | 1.02 | 1.41 |
| M/B | 0.94 | 0.93 |
| M/C | 1.03 | 0.54 (undercharged) |

This is the pricing version of Chouldechova's result: when base rates
differ, parity and calibration cannot both hold.

### 5.5 Robustness — full 100K dataset

All numbers above use the committed 10K sample. Re-running the identical
pipeline on the full 100K dataset (reproducible via the generator, seed 42):

| Metric (100K) | Value |
|---|---:|
| Segment calibration range | 0.98–1.02 |
| Holdout loss ratio | 0.98 |
| Full segment parity cost | +1.8% MSE |
| Premium volume moved at full parity | 34.8% |
| Loss-ratio gap at full parity | 1.36 |
| Gender parity cost | +0.25% MSE |
| Gender parity volume moved | 17.1% |

The frontier has the same shape at 100K; small differences from the 10K
sample are sampling noise, not model instability.

## 6. The impossibility result

For two groups A, B with base rates `p_A ≠ p_B`:

* **Calibration** requires `P(Y=1 | S=s)` equal across groups for every
  score `s`.
* **Equalized odds** requires the score distributions of true positives and
  true negatives to match across groups.

Bayes' rule couples the two through the base rate. In the sample, matching
true-positive rates requires thresholds 0.131 (F) vs 0.164 (M) — a single
score cannot be both calibrated and group-fair. The demonstration is in
[`notebooks/03_fairness.ipynb`](../notebooks/03_fairness.ipynb).

## 7. Limitations

* **Synthetic data.** The ground truth is known, which is the point — but
  the absolute numbers are illustrative. Methods transfer to real data; the
  frontier's shape, not its dollar values, is the result.
* **Segment multipliers.** Post-processing with segment-level multipliers is
  one family of constrained-pricing methods; per-policy optimization or
  model-level constraints would move the curve.
* **One fairness definition.** We implement demographic parity on premiums.
  Equalized odds on claim rates, calibration constraints, or individual
  fairness would give different frontiers.
* **MSE as accuracy.** Mean squared error weights large losses heavily; a
  loss-ratio or deviance measure would change the cost axis.
